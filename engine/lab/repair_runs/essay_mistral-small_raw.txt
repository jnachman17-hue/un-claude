The London establishments where coffee was served served purposes far beyond caffeine. Within sixty years of the very first shop’s debut in 1652, every ward in the city housed such spots, each drawing its own cluster of patrons: mariners at one, writers at another, financiers at a third. In 1698 the financiers were barred from the Royal Exchange yet carried on their dealings in those coffee sites for another eighty years.

One scholar’s description likened the venue to “the internet of its era,” a hub where reports, gossip and price data raced quicker than anywhere else across Europe. The novelty of this arrangement is difficult to exaggerate. A merchant’s trainee might share a table with a parliamentary delegate and both absorb identical tidings at the same instant.

The takeaway for 2028 shows not that tools reprise their roles, but that venues where data converges dictate who benefits from it. This held in 1698, and it remains the case today.
