No one ever went to London’s coffee houses just for the coffee. By sixty years after the first appeared in 1652, they had spread to every district, each drawing its own regulars—sailors in one, writers in another, traders in yet another. When those traders were barred from the Royal Exchange in 1698, they didn’t stop; they simply took their business to the coffee houses and kept at it for the following eighty years.

One scholar called the coffee house "the internet of its day"—a spot where updates, gossip, and market figures circulated more swiftly than anywhere else on the continent. The novelty of this can’t be exaggerated. An apprentice clerk might find himself next to a lawmaker, both catching the same bulletins in real time.

The takeaway for 2028 isn’t that history’s gadgets repeat themselves. It’s that the places where knowledge gathers determine who gets to turn it into wealth. The rule held in 1698, and it hasn’t changed since.
