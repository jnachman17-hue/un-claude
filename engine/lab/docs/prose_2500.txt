The argument for reading slowly has never been fashionable, and it is easy to see why. Speed feels like competence. A person who finishes a book in a weekend appears to have got more out of it than someone still circling the same chapter on Thursday. But the appearance is misleading, and the reason it misleads is that reading is not a transfer of information from a page into a head. It is closer to a conversation, and conversations conducted at speed tend to be the ones where nobody hears anything.

Consider what actually happens when a difficult paragraph works on you. You read it, and something snags. You go back. The second pass is different from the first because you now know where the sentence is going, so you can attend to how it gets there. On the third pass you start arguing with it. None of this can be compressed. The snagging is the point, and a reader who never snags has not read faster, they have read less.

This is why the productivity framing corrupts the activity it claims to improve. Once a book becomes a task with a completion state, everything that resists completion starts to look like friction to be removed. Difficulty becomes a defect in the writing rather than a demand on the reader. And the books that make the largest demands, which are generally the books worth the time, are exactly the ones this framing teaches you to abandon.

The counterargument is real and deserves stating. Most of what anyone reads does not reward slowness. A great deal of writing is padded, repetitive, or simply not very good, and treating it with reverence is its own kind of error. The skill worth having is not slowness but discrimination: knowing which pages deserve a second pass and which deserve to be skimmed and forgotten. That skill, unhelpfully, cannot be taught in the abstract. It comes from having read enough to recognise the difference.

What follows is not a method. Methods are the problem. But there is a habit worth trying, which is to read with a pencil and to write in the margins as though replying to someone. Not summarising, which is passive, but answering. Disagreeing where you disagree. The point is not the notes, most of which you will never reread. The point is that answering requires understanding, and the pencil catches you out whenever you have been nodding along without having followed anything at all.
