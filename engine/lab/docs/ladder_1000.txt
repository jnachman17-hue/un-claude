1. Fairmont: School start times

Fairmont changed school start times in September 2016, and the effect on adolescent attendance was larger than anything the authority had funded in the preceding decade. In the first full year adolescent attendance moved by 8 percent against a comparison group of 15 similar districts, and the gap had not closed three years later. The figure is not in dispute; what the figure is worth is.

Smith (2018) put the result in the plainest terms available at the time, and the sentence has been quoted in every business case written since: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). The claim survived two replications and one failed replication in a district that had already made a timetable change of its own.

The 2018 review of 23 districts reached the same conclusion by a different route. Its authors concluded that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a sentence that has been quoted by both sides of the argument since, usually with the second clause removed. What the review actually recommended was a staged rollout with a control group, which no authority has yet attempted.

The dissent is worth stating in full, because it is normally paraphrased into something weaker. The minority report recorded:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The transport ledger tells the other half of the story, and it is the half that arrives first. Fairmont runs its service in two tiers, and the change pushed the second tier into the same window as the first. The authority bought 4 vehicles and hired 6 staff, and the transport line rose by about 10 percent in the first year and by a further 4 percent in the second. None of that appears in the adolescent attendance figures, and all of it appears in the budget papers.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance officer said, and nobody at the meeting argued the point. A second officer observed that the envelope had been empty since 2012. Neither remark made the minutes, which recorded only that the committee had noted the report.

What the 2022 follow-up added was duration. The effect on adolescent attendance was undiminished at three years in 10 of the 15 districts, which is the finding that matters for a business case and the one most often left out of the summary. The transport increase was also undiminished. A policy that buys a durable gain with a durable cost is a different proposition from one that buys a durable gain with a one-off cost, and the papers rarely say which is which.

2. Kelso County: Rural broadband subsidy

Conclusion

The adolescent attendance result is real, the duration result is real, and the transport cost is real. The only dishonest position is the one that mentions two of the three. Authorities weighing the change should price the transport line first, because it is the part that arrives on the first morning and never goes away.
