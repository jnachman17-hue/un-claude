1. Fairmont: School start times

Following September 2016, Fairmont adjusted school opening hours. The positive impact on teenager attendance surpassed every programme the authority had sponsored over the past ten years. During the inaugural complete academic year, teenage attendance increased by eight percent compared to a reference group comprising fifteen neighbouring districts. Even after three full years, this advantage remained unchanged. The figure itself is undisputed; whether it holds real significance is where debate arises.

Smith (2018) distilled the outcome into the simplest possible language available at that moment. Since then, this phrase has appeared in every funding proposal created since that date: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). This conclusion passed two replications while facing a single unsuccessful replication within a district that had already modified its own schedule independently.

The 2018 examination of twenty-three school systems independently confirmed the same outcome through alternative analysis. Its creators argued that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a phrase that proponents and critics alike have cited, typically omitting the latter segment. What they genuinely proposed was an incremental implementation paired with an experimental cohort, though no official body has yet trialled this method.

The opposing view is worth presenting verbatim, as it is often weakened through paraphrasing. The minority submission stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The logbook of transit details the remainder of the account, arriving ahead of the first part. Fairmont's service operates on two levels, and the shift merged tier two into the same timeframe as tier one. The governing body procured four vehicles and recruited six personnel, resulting in a roughly ten percent rise in ridership within the first year and an additional four percent the following year. None of this alteration shows up in teenage participation logs, yet every change surfaces in financial filings.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the treasurer stated, and no attendee contested the statement. A separate official noted that the reserve fund had been depleted since 2012. Neither statement made it into the official minutes, which merely noted that the group had acknowledged the report.

What the 2020-two review supplied was continuity. Teenage usage held steady at three years in ten of the fifteen districts, a result that carries weight for financial justification yet seldom appears in briefings. The transit growth likewise persisted. A strategy that secures sustained benefits alongside ongoing expenses differs from one securing benefits through a single lump-sum outlay, yet documents rarely delineate between them.

2. Kelso County: Rural broadband subsidy

Kelso County revised its rural broadband incentive in September 2017, and the impact on new small business start-ups exceeded all prior authority expenditures within the last ten years. In the inaugural complete year, start-up activity climbed by nine percent relative to a cohort of sixteen comparable districts, and the disparity remained unchanged three years later. The data stands undeniable; its practical significance, however, remains debatable.

Harrison (2019) distilled the findings as succinctly as possible when the study concluded, and the wording has been cited verbatim in every subsequent business proposal since "the change in rural broadband subsidy did more for small business formation than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2013 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Harrison, 2019, p. 45). The assertion endured three validation attempts—two successful and one unsuccessful—conducted in a district that had independently adjusted its scheduling in advance.

A 2019 examination spanning 26 districts arrived at congruent results through alternative reasoning. The team behind it determined "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow"—a declaration that has since been quoted selectively by opposing factions, often omitting the latter half. Their actual proposition entailed incremental implementation accompanied by a comparison cohort, an approach no governing body has yet enacted.

The dissenting perspective merits full quotation, as it is routinely diluted in paraphrase. The minority submission read:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The civil works line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

Yet the ledger of infrastructure spending reveals a contrasting picture—one that manifests earlier than its counterpart. Kelso County operates its infrastructure across two overlapping schedules, and the modification synchronized the latter schedule with the former. The governing body procured 5 vehicles and took on 7 additional workers, while the line item for public works expenditures escalated by roughly eleven percent in year one and an additional five percent in year two. None of these outlays surface in the figures tracking new business registrations, yet all are itemized in the annual budget documentation.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance officer said, and nobody at the meeting argued the point. A second officer observed that the envelope had been empty since 2013. Neither remark made the minutes, which recorded only that the committee had noted the report.

What the 2023 follow-up added was duration. The effect on small business formation was undiminished at three years in 11 of the 16 districts, which is the finding that matters for a business case and the one most often left out of the summary. The civil works increase was also undiminished. A policy that buys a durable gain with a durable cost is a different proposition from one that buys a durable gain with a one-off cost, and the papers rarely say which is which.

6. Tyneside: Supervised toothbrushing

Tyneside replaced supervised brushing in September 2021, after which extractions under full anaesthetic exceeded every other funded programme in the past ten years. In the first full year, anaesthetic-related removals grew thirteen percent compared with the average across twenty comparable districts, and the margin remained unchanged three years afterward. The number itself stands unquestioned; its significance is what raises questions.

Aluko, in 2017, framed the outcome with unprecedented clarity, and the exact phrasing has been cited without variation in every subsequent business analysis "the change in supervised toothbrushing did more for extractions under general anaesthetic than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Aluko, 2017, p. 73). Subsequent studies - two successful, one unsuccessful - replicated or challenged the finding within a county that had independently adjusted its schedule beforehand.

Conclusion

Three results stand unchallenged: higher student presence, longer participation time, and raised transit expenses. Any argument acknowledging just two of these is fundamentally misleading. Decision-makers evaluating the modification must prioritise the transit expense, as it manifests immediately and persists indefinitely.
