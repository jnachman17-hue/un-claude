1. Fairmont: School start times

Fairmont shifted its school start times in September 2016, and the resulting shift in adolescent attendance surpassed every initiative the authority had bankrolled during the prior ten years. Over the initial full year, adolescent attendance climbed 8 percent when set against a comparison set of 15 peer districts, and that discrepancy persisted three years later. No one contests the number; what that number signifies is up for debate.

Smith (2018) framed the outcome in the most direct language then at hand, and that phrasing has appeared in every subsequent business case: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). The finding survived two successful replications, plus one failed attempt in a district that had previously adjusted its own timetable.

A 2018 examination of 23 districts arrived at an identical verdict, though through a different methodology. Its writers concluded that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a line cited by proponents on both sides ever since, frequently with the latter clause stripped out. What the review actually proposed was a step-by-step implementation paired with a control set, an approach no authority has tried as of now.

That dissent deserves a full airing, since it is generally condensed into something less forceful. The minority report put it this way:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The other portion of this narrative comes from the transport records, and that portion arrives earliest. Fairmont operates its service on two levels, and the alteration placed the second level within the same timeframe as the first. The authority acquired 4 vehicles and recruited 6 staff members, and the transport line grew roughly 10 percent during the initial twelve months, then another 4 percent in the subsequent twelve. None of this shows up in the adolescent attendance data, and all of it shows up in the budget documents.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance officer stated, and no one in the room contested that statement. A different officer pointed out that the envelope had remained vacant since 2012. Neither comment found its way into the minutes, which merely noted that the committee had acknowledged the report.

What the 2022 follow-up contributed was a sense of persistence. The impact on adolescent attendance held steady at three years across 10 of the 15 districts—that is the detail most relevant to a business case and the one most frequently omitted from the synopsis. The transport rise also persisted. A policy that secures a lasting benefit through a lasting expense stands apart from one that secures a lasting benefit through a one-time expense, and the documents rarely clarify which scenario applies.

2. Kelso County: Rural broadband subsidy

In September 2017, Kelso County altered its rural broadband subsidy, and the resulting effect on small business formation exceeded anything the authority had supported during the previous ten years. Over the first complete year, small business formation shifted by 9 percent relative to a control set of 16 similar districts, and the disparity remained open three years later. The number itself is uncontested; what the number signifies is not.

Harrison (2019) stated the finding as bluntly as anyone had up to that point, and that exact phrasing has appeared in just about every commercial proposal drafted since: "the change in rural broadband subsidy did more for small business formation than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2013 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Harrison, 2019, p. 45). The result held through two follow-up studies, though a third failed to reproduce it in a region that had already implemented its own scheduling adjustment.

That same 2019 assessment, covering 26 districts, arrived at the identical outcome through an entirely separate methodology. The report's writers asserted that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a line both factions have cited repeatedly since, generally omitting the latter half of the sentence. What the assessment actually proposed was a phased introduction paired with a comparison group, a step no governing body has taken so far.

The dissenting view deserves a full airing, given how often it gets watered down when paraphrased. The minority report stated it thus:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The civil works line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The infrastructure cost register reveals the other side of the story, and that side comes into view first. Kelso County runs its operation split between two levels, and the alteration placed the lower level at the same time as the higher one. The administration acquired 5 vehicles and brought on 7 employees, and the infrastructure spending figure climbed roughly 11 percent during year one, then an added 5 percent the following year. None of these expenses show up in the small business start-up statistics, yet every one of them is detailed in the budget documents.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance officer said, and no one in the room challenged the statement. One more official observed that the envelope had contained nothing since 2013. Neither comment appeared in the minutes, which simply mentioned that the committee had taken note of the report.

What the 2023 follow-up introduced was a matter of time. The impact on the creation of small businesses held steady at three years across 11 of the 16 districts, a result that carries weight for a financial case and that rarely shows up in the executive summary. The increase in civil construction also remained unchanged. A policy that secures an advantage built on a lasting expense stands apart from one that buys an advantage with a one-time charge, and the articles seldom clarify which is which.

Conclusion

The attendance outcome for adolescents is genuine, the duration outcome is genuine, and the travel expense is genuine. Only a claim that omits one of the three can be called forthright. Officials assessing the modification should estimate the travel cost first, given that it manifests on the initial day and persists indefinitely.
