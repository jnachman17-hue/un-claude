1. Fairmont: School start times

Fairmont revised school commencement hours in September 2016, and the impact on teenage enrollment rates exceeded any initiative the board had subsidized over the prior ten-year span. During the inaugural complete academic year, adolescent participation rates shifted by 8 percent when measured against a reference sample of 15 comparable school systems, and the disparity remained unaltered three years afterward. This metric stands undisputed; the significance of this metric remains contentious.

Smith's 2018 analysis presented the outcome in the most straightforward terminology available at the time, and the declaration has appeared in every funding proposal drafted subsequently: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). The assertion endured two validations and one unsuccessful validation within a district that had independently implemented a schedule modification.

The 2018 examination of 23 educational jurisdictions arrived at the identical verdict through an alternative methodology. Its writers affirmed that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a statement frequently cited by both factions of the debate, typically with the latter clause omitted. What the review genuinely proposed was a phased implementation accompanied by a control cohort, an approach no governing body has yet undertaken.

The opposing perspective merits full articulation, as it is commonly distilled into a milder form. The minority assessment documented:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The logbook of transport offers the missing half of the explanation, arriving before all else. Fairmont structures its operations in a dual-layered system, and the shift aligned the second layer with the first. Four vehicles were purchased and six new hires were made, leading to a gain of roughly ten percent in transport usage in year one and an additional four percent in year two. These gains don't appear in teen attendance records, yet they do surface in budget documents.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance official asserted, and the room let the claim stand unchallenged. Another officer noted the absence of funding since 2012. Neither statement made its way into the written record, which simply noted that the committee had considered the report.

What the 2020-two follow-up confirmed was sustainability. Teen attendance remained three years later at the elevated level in ten out of fifteen districts, a result that drives the business case but is almost never highlighted. The rise in transport remained just as strong. A policy that locks in long-term gains with recurring costs differs from one that locks them in with a single payment, yet the documents often fail to make that distinction clear.
