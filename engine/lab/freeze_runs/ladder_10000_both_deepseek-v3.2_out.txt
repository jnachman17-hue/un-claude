1. Fairmont: School start times

Fairmont shifted its school start times in September 2016, and the boost to adolescent attendance outpaced anything the authority had paid for across the prior ten years. During the initial full year, adolescent attendance rose 8 percent when measured against a control set of 15 comparable districts, and that difference persisted three years later. Nobody questions the figure; the debate is over what it truly signifies.

Smith (2018) phrased the finding as directly as possible at the time, and that phrasing has appeared in every subsequent funding proposal: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). That assertion held up through two successful replications, plus one failed attempt in a district that had already adjusted its own schedule.

The 2018 evaluation covering 23 districts arrived at an identical finding through a different path. Its writers determined that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a line both supporters and critics have cited since, frequently dropping the final phrase. The review's actual suggestion was a phased implementation with a comparison arm, which no system has tried to date.

The minority opinion deserves full quotation, because it often gets summarized into something softer. That dissenting statement read:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The transport ledger tells the other half of the story, and it is the half that arrives first. The service is operated by Fairmont in two distinct levels, and the change moved the lower level into the same timeframe as the upper one. The authority acquired 4 vehicles and added 6 staff, and the transport line went up by about 10 percent over the initial twelve months and then by another 4 percent in the following year. None of this shows up in the adolescent attendance numbers, and every bit of it is visible in the budget documents.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance officer stated, and no one at the meeting raised a objection. A different officer pointed out that the envelope had been void since 2012. Neither comment made it into the minutes, which merely noted that the committee had taken the report under consideration.

What the 2022 follow-up contributed was a sense of persistence. The impact on adolescent attendance stayed strong at three years in 10 of the 15 districts, and that is the detail that matters for a business case and the one most frequently omitted from the overview. The transport growth also held steady. A policy that buys a lasting advantage with an ongoing expense is a very different thing from one that buys a lasting advantage with a single payment, and the documents seldom clarify which type is at hand.

During the initial twelve months, housing turnover changed by 20 percent when set against 27 comparable local authorities, and the difference remained unclosed even three years on. The statistic itself faces no challenge; what that statistic signifies is another matter.

Okonkwo (2018) framed the finding in the most straightforward language then possible, and that particular line has appeared in each subsequent proposal document: "the change in the vacant property levy did more for housing turnover than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2014 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Okonkwo, 2018, p. 122). The conclusion withstood two independent checks and one unsuccessful repetition in a region that had already enacted its own schedule adjustment.

The earlier examination, covering 59 authorities in 2018, arrived at the identical finding via an alternative pathway. Its writers determined that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a phrase cited by supporters as well as critics ever since, typically omitting the latter half. What the report genuinely suggested was a phased introduction alongside a benchmark set of regions, an approach that no council has tried to date.

The objection merits a complete presentation, given how frequently it gets softened in retelling. The dissenting statement put forward:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The enforcement line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The enforcement ledger tells the other half of the story, and it is the half that arrives first. Ardwick runs its service in two tiers, and the change pushed the second tier into the same window as the first. The authority bought 6 vehicles and hired 9 staff, and the enforcement line rose by about 22 percent in the first year and by a further 4 percent in the second. None of that appears in the housing turnover figures, and all of it appears in the budget papers.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance officer said, and nobody at the meeting argued the point. A second officer observed that the envelope had been empty since 2014. Neither remark made the minutes, which recorded only that the committee had noted the report.

What the 2022 follow-up added was duration. The effect on housing turnover was undiminished at three years in 10 of the 27 districts, which is the finding that matters for a business case and the one most often left out of the summary. The enforcement increase was also undiminished. A policy that buys a durable gain with a durable cost is a different proposition from one that buys a durable gain with a one-off cost, and the papers rarely say which is which.

Conclusion

The outcome around adolescent presence is genuine, the outcome around duration is genuine, and the expenditure on travel is genuine. Solely the stance that references two out of those three is false. Officials contemplating the adjustment ought to evaluate the transportation expense initially, since it is the element manifesting on day one and persisting indefinitely.
