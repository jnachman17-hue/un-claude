1. Fairmont: School start times

In September 2016, Fairmont adjusted its school schedules, producing an impact on teen attendance more significant than any initiative the district had backed in the prior ten years. During the first complete academic year, attendance among adolescents shifted by 8 percent compared to a benchmark group of 15 comparable districts—and that difference persisted even after three years had passed. No one contests the data; the debate centers on what it signifies.

Smith's 2018 analysis framed the outcome with blunt clarity, crafting a line that has since become a staple in every funding proposal: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). That assertion held up through two independent validations and one instance where replication faltered in a district that had independently altered its own schedule beforehand.

A 2018 assessment covering 23 districts arrived at an identical verdict through a separate method. The researchers behind it wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow"—a statement frequently cited by advocates and critics alike, though often stripped of its second half. Their actual advice called for a phased implementation paired with a control group, an approach no district has put into practice so far.

The opposing view deserves to be presented exactly as written, since summaries tend to dilute its force. The dissenting report stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

Here's the rest of the picture—and it was the first part to become clear. Fairmont's operations split into two distinct levels, but the adjustment meant both now functioned on the same schedule. The agency acquired 4 new vehicles and took on 6 additional employees, which sent the transport line climbing by roughly 10 percent in year one and another 4 percent in year two. Those shifts don't show up in the youth participation data, though they're all accounted for in the financial records.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," The treasurer made the point outright, and no one in the room challenged it. Another official noted that the funding pool had been drained since 2012. Neither statement ended up in the official minutes, which simply stated that the committee had acknowledged the report.

The 2022 update introduced a critical detail: time. In 10 out of the 15 districts, the impact on youth participation held steady after three years—that's the metric that carries weight in cost-benefit analysis, yet it's the one summaries tend to omit. The transport expansion also endured. A program that delivers lasting benefits at a lasting expense is fundamentally different from one that achieves the same with a single payment, but the documents seldom clarify which scenario applies.
