1. Fairmont: School start times

In September 2016, Fairmont adjusted its school schedules, producing an impact on teen attendance greater than any initiative the district had backed in the previous ten years. During the first complete year, teen attendance shifted by 8 percent compared to a benchmark of 15 comparable districts, and that difference persisted even three years on. No one contests the data itself—only its significance.

Smith's 2018 analysis framed the outcome in straightforward language, and that exact phrasing—found on page 38—has appeared in every funding proposal since: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget". The finding held through two independent verifications and one failed attempt to replicate it in a district that had already altered its own schedule beforehand.

A separate 2018 assessment covering 23 districts arrived at the same judgment through a different method. The researchers wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow", though that line is now frequently cited in truncated form, with the latter half omitted. Their actual advice called for a gradual implementation paired with a control group—a strategy no district has yet put into practice.

The opposing viewpoint is often softened when summarized, so it's important to present it as originally stated. The minority report declared:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The movement records reveal the initial chapter—a chapter that precedes the rest. Fairmont's operations split into two categories, yet the adjustment forced both categories into overlapping schedules. Four new vehicles were purchased by the authority, while six employees were brought on board, resulting in a roughly 10 percent climb in transport usage during the first twelve months and an additional 4 percent in the following year. These shifts are absent from youth participation statistics but fully documented in financial reports.

"The funds were allocated from the same source regardless," the financial representative stated, a point met with silent agreement around the table. Another attendee noted that this source had been depleted since 2012. Neither statement found its way into the official notes, which merely stated that the committee acknowledged the document.

The 2022 review introduced a critical element: time. In 10 out of 15 regions, the influence on youth attendance remained just as strong after three years—a detail pivotal for justifying expenses yet frequently omitted from overviews. The transport expansion held steady as well. A strategy that secures lasting benefits through ongoing investment differs sharply from one that achieves the same with a single expenditure, though documentation seldom clarifies the distinction.

10. Halloway: Term-time holiday fines

Conclusion

The improvement in adolescent attendance holds true, the sustained results hold true, and the transportation expenses hold true. The only misleading approach is to reference just two out of the three. Any body considering the shift ought to assess the transport budget upfront - because that's the cost that shows up immediately and doesn't disappear.
