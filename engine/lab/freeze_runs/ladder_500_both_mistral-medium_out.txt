1. Fairmont: School start times

The shift in school schedules Fairmont introduced in September 2016 produced a bigger impact on teen attendance than any other initiative the district had backed in the past ten years. Over the following twelve months, attendance among adolescents rose by 8 percent relative to a matched group of 15 comparable districts—and that advantage persisted unchanged even after three years. No one contests the measurement itself; the debate centers on what it truly signifies.

Smith's 2018 analysis framed the finding in straightforward language, producing a line so widely cited that it now appears in every subsequent funding proposal: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). That assertion held up through two independent validations and one inconclusive test in a district where schedules had already been adjusted independently.

A separate 2018 examination of 23 districts arrived at an identical verdict through a different method. Its researchers wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" - a statement both supporters and critics have since invoked, though usually truncating it after the first clause. The review's actual advice called for a gradual, controlled implementation with a comparison group, a recommendation that remains untested by any district to date.

The opposing perspective deserves to be presented exactly as written, since summaries tend to dilute its force. The minority report's exact wording was:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The movement records reveal the earlier chapter of what unfolded - one that arrives ahead of the rest. Fairmont operates on a dual system, and the adjustment forced both levels to overlap in timing. Officials acquired 4 new vehicles and took on 6 employees, which drove transport expenses up by roughly 10 percent in the opening year and an additional 4 percent in the following one. These shifts don't show up in the youth participation data, but they're fully accounted for in the financial reports.

Conclusion

The gains in adolescent attendance hold true, the timeframe outcomes hold true, and the transport expenses hold true. The sole misleading approach is to highlight just two while ignoring the third. Any group evaluating the shift ought to calculate the transport burden upfront - it's the cost that materializes immediately and persists without end.
