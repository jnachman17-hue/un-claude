1. Fairmont: School start times

In September 2016, Fairmont adjusted when schools began, and the shift in teen attendance rates surpassed the impact of any other initiative the district had backed in the prior ten years. Over the first complete academic year, teen attendance shifted by 8 percent relative to a benchmark of 15 comparable districts—and that advantage persisted unchanged even after three years. No one contests the measurement; the debate centers on its significance.

Smith's 2018 analysis framed the outcome with unmatched clarity, producing a line that has since become a staple in every subsequent funding proposal: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). That assertion held firm through two independent validations and one inconclusive attempt in a district where schedules had already been revised beforehand.

A separate 2018 assessment covering 23 districts arrived at an identical verdict through a distinct approach. The researchers behind it wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" - a statement now routinely cited by advocates and critics alike, though often stripped of its qualifying second half. Their actual advice called for a gradual implementation paired with a monitored comparison group, a strategy no district has put into practice so far.

The opposing viewpoint deserves to be presented exactly as written, since summaries tend to dilute its force. The dissenting document stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The movement records reveal what came before - and it's the part that hits immediately. Fairmont's operations split into two distinct levels, yet the adjustment forced both to operate within the same timeframe. Officials acquired 4 new vehicles and brought on 6 additional employees, triggering a roughly 10% increase in transport expenses during the first twelve months, followed by an extra 4% in the second. These shifts don't surface in the youth participation data, but they're plainly documented in the financial reports.

Conclusion

The improvements in adolescent attendance are genuine, the time-based outcomes hold up, and the rise in transport spending is undeniable. The deception lies in citing only two out of the three. Any entity considering this shift ought to assess the transport budget upfront - it's the expense that materializes at dawn on day one and never fades.
