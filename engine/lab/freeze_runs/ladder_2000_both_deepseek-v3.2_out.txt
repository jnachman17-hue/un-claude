1. Fairmont: School start times

In September 2016, Fairmont shifted its school start times, and the resulting improvement in attendance among teens outpaced any initiative the authority had bankrolled in the ten years prior. During the initial twelve months, teen attendance climbed by 8 percent when set against a benchmark of 15 comparable districts, and that divergence persisted even three years later. Everyone agrees on the number; what that number means is another matter entirely.

The way Smith (2018) phrased the finding was as direct as anything then in print, and that sentence has appeared in every funding proposal drafted since then: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). The finding held up through two independent checks and one unsuccessful check in a district that had already altered its own schedule in advance.

In 2018, a comprehensive assessment of 23 districts arrived at the same verdict through a separate path. Those who wrote it concluded that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — and this phrasing has been invoked by supporters and critics ever since, often stripped of its second half. The true suggestion from that report was a phased implementation paired with a control set, an approach no authority has yet attempted.

It's worthwhile to lay out the objection in complete form, because it's commonly softened through paraphrase. The minority statement said:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The transport ledger reveals the other side of the narrative, and that side makes its appearance first. Fairmont operates its service across two distinct levels, and the adjustment forced the lower level into the same period as the upper one. The authority acquired 4 vehicles and brought on 6 staff members, with the transport line climbing roughly 10 percent during the initial year and then another 4 percent in the following one. None of this shows up in the teenage attendance data, while all of it is visible in the budget records.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance officer stated, and no one in the room challenged that assessment. A different officer pointed out that the fund had remained unfilled going back to 2012. Neither comment found its way into the minutes, which simply noted that the committee had acknowledged the report.

What the 2022 assessment contributed was a sense of endurance. The impact on teenage attendance held steady without weakening after three years in 10 of the 15 districts, a result that holds significance for establishing a business case and is also the one frequently omitted from any recap. The transport growth likewise persisted at its prior level. A policy that secures an abiding gain at an abiding expense stands apart from one that secures an abiding gain with a single payment, and the documentation rarely distinguishes the two.

Conclusion

The attendance figure for teenagers holds up, the time frame holds up, and the commuting expense holds up. Claiming just two of these three is the sole misleading stance. Officials deciding on the adjustment ought to cost out the travel route initially, since that element shows up on day one and persists indefinitely.
