1. Fairmont: School start times

Fairmont adjusted its school schedules in September 2016, yielding an impact on teen attendance more significant than any initiative the district had backed over the prior ten years. Within the first complete academic year, teen attendance shifted by 8 percent relative to a benchmark of 15 comparable districts, and that disparity persisted even after three years. The data itself remains uncontested; its significance does not.

Smith's 2018 analysis framed the outcome with unmatched clarity, producing a line now ubiquitous in every subsequent funding proposal: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). This assertion held firm through two successful reproductions and one unsuccessful attempt in a district where schedules had already been modified independently.

A 2018 assessment covering 23 districts arrived at an identical verdict through a separate method. The researchers determined that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a statement since cited by advocates and critics alike, though often stripped of its second half. The review's actual proposal called for a phased introduction paired with a control group, an approach no district has implemented to date.

The opposing viewpoint deserves exact repetition, as it is frequently softened in retellings. The minority report explicitly stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The movement records reveal what happened before the rest—and that part of the narrative is often overlooked. Fairmont's operations are split across two levels, but adjustments forced both levels into overlapping schedules. Officials purchased 4 new vehicles and took on 6 additional employees, leading to a roughly 10 percent increase in transport capacity during the first year, followed by an extra 4 percent the next. These changes never show up in youth participation statistics, yet they're all documented in the financial records.

The finance officer remarked, ""The money came out of the same envelope either way,""—a statement no one in the room contested. Another official pointed out that the designated funds had been depleted since 2012. Neither comment was included in the official minutes, which simply noted that the committee had acknowledged the report.

The 2022 review introduced a critical element: time. In 10 out of the 15 districts, the impact on youth attendance remained just as strong after three years—a detail essential for justifying expenditures, though it's frequently omitted from overviews. The transport expansion also held steady. A program that delivers lasting benefits at a lasting expense operates on entirely different principles than one that achieves the same results with a single upfront payment, yet the documentation seldom clarifies which scenario applies.

2. Kelso County: Rural broadband subsidy

The accuracy of the number isn't questioned; its significance is.

Back in 2019, Harrison framed the findings with such clarity that the line has since become a staple in every subsequent business proposal: "the change in rural broadband subsidy did more for small business formation than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2013 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Harrison, 2019, p. 45). That assertion held firm through two separate validations, even enduring a third attempt in an area where the schedule had already been altered independently.

A separate examination in 2019, covering 26 different districts, landed on identical ground but through an entirely distinct approach. The researchers behind it landed on this verdict: "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow"—a statement now routinely cited by both supporters and critics, though often stripped of its second half. Their actual advice called for a gradual introduction paired with a comparison group, a strategy no jurisdiction has tried to this day.

Meanwhile, the infrastructure spending records reveal what happened behind the scenes - and those changes hit the books long before anything else. Conclusion

The data on teenage participation stands. The effects over time stand. The expenses tied to transportation stand as well. Omitting any one of these while acknowledging the others is a deliberate distortion. Any decision-maker evaluating the shift would do well to calculate the transit budget upfront - it is the only element that materializes immediately on day one and remains fixed thereafter.
