1. Fairmont: School start times

In September 2016, Fairmont adjusted its school schedules, producing a shift in teenage attendance unlike any initiative the district had backed in the prior ten years. Over the initial twelve months, teenage participation rose by 8 percent relative to a benchmark of 15 comparable districts—a difference that persisted even after three years had passed. No one contests the data; the debate centers on its significance.

Smith's 2018 analysis framed the outcome with unmatched clarity, delivering a line that now appears in every subsequent funding proposal: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). That assertion held firm through two independent validations and one counterexample in a district where schedules had already been revised beforehand.

A separate 2018 examination of 23 districts arrived at an identical verdict through a distinct approach. The researchers determined that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow"—a statement both supporters and critics have since invoked, though typically omitting the latter half. The review's actual suggestion was a phased introduction paired with a monitored comparison group, an approach no district has tried to date.

The opposing perspective deserves full presentation, as it is often diluted in retellings. The dissenting document explicitly noted:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The movement records reveal the earlier chapter of this account. Fairmont's operations are divided into two levels, and the adjustment forced both levels into overlapping schedules. Four new vehicles were purchased by the authority, along with six new employees, resulting in a roughly 10 percent increase in transport capacity during the first year and an additional 4 percent in the second. These details are absent from the youth participation statistics but fully documented in the financial reports.

"Funding was drawn from the same source regardless," the financial representative stated—a point no one in the session contested. Another official noted that this source had been exhausted since 2012. Neither statement was included in the official minutes, which merely stated that the committee acknowledged the report.

The 2022 review introduced a critical factor: time. In 10 out of 15 regions, the impact on youth participation remained unchanged after three years—a key insight for justifying expenditures and one frequently omitted from summaries. The transport expansion also persisted. A strategy that secures lasting benefits through ongoing investment differs fundamentally from one that achieves the same with a single payment, yet the documentation seldom clarifies this distinction.

Conclusion

The evidence confirms three undeniable effects: improved teenage participation, extended operational hours, and escalated transportation expenses. Omitting any one of these while discussing the others constitutes a deliberate misrepresentation. Decision-makers evaluating the shift must prioritize calculating the transit budget upfront, as this is the immediate, permanent cost that emerges from day one.
