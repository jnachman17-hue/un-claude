1. Fairmont: School start times

Fairmont shifted its school starting times in September 2016, and the boost to adolescent attendance proved larger than any initiative the authority had bankrolled over the previous ten years. During the initial twelve months, attendance among adolescents advanced 8 percent when set against a benchmark of 15 comparable districts, and three years later the difference remained. Nobody argues about the number; what the number signifies is another matter.

In Smith (2018), the outcome was phrased as simply as could be managed then, and that phrasing has appeared in every subsequent budget proposal: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). That claim held up through two independent checks and a third that didn't match, in a district that had already altered its own schedule.

A 2018 assessment covering 23 districts arrived at the same verdict via alternate means. The authors decided that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a line cited since by advocates on both sides, typically with the latter part omitted. What the assessment truly proposed was a phased implementation with a control group, something no authority has attempted to date.

The objection deserves a complete restatement, since it is usually condensed into something tamer. The minority report put it this way:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The other side of the situation is captured in the transport records, and that's the side that shows up earliest. Fairmont's service operates on two levels, and the adjustment placed the lower tier squarely within the timeframe of the upper one. The authority purchased 4 vehicles, brought on 6 staff members, and the transport expenditure grew roughly 10 percent across the initial twelve months, then another 4 percent the following year. None of that shows up in the adolescent attendance data, yet every bit of it is visible in the budget documents.

Conclusion

The adolescent attendance figure is genuine, the duration figure is genuine, and the transport expense is genuine. The only misleading stance is one that brings up just two of these three. Those evaluating the change ought to calculate the transport expense first, seeing as it's the component that's present from the first morning and remains indefinitely.
