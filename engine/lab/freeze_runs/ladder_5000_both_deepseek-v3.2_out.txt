1. Fairmont: School start times

Fairmont shifted its school start times in September 2016, and the boost to adolescent attendance proved greater than any program the authority had funded during the prior ten years. Across the initial full year, adolescent attendance gained 8 percent relative to a control set of 15 comparable districts, and that margin persisted three years onward. No one argues with the number; the argument is over what the number signifies.

Smith (2018) framed the finding as plainly as possible at that moment, and that phrasing has appeared in every proposal drafted since: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). The result withstood two successful replications plus one null result in a district that had already adjusted its own schedule in advance.

The 2018 assessment covering 23 districts arrived at a similar verdict through a distinct methodology. The writers determined that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a phrasing cited by proponents and critics alike since then, typically with the latter half omitted. What the evaluation advocated in practice was a phased implementation featuring a control arm, an approach no authority has attempted so far.

The opposition deserves to be presented in full, given that it often gets condensed into gentler language. The dissenting statement read:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The other side of the story is told by the transport ledger, and it's the side that shows up first. Fairmont operates its service on two levels, and the shift caused the lower level to overlap with the upper one's timeframe. Four vehicles were purchased by the authority, and 6 people were brought on staff; the transport line rose around 10 percent during the initial twelve months and then an extra 4 percent the year after. None of that shows up in the adolescent attendance numbers, while all of it is visible in the budget documents.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance officer said, and no one at the session disputed it. A different officer pointed out that the envelope had remained vacant since 2012. Both remarks were absent from the minutes, which only noted that the report had been acknowledged by the committee.

What the 2022 follow-up contributed was a sense of endurance. In 10 of the 15 districts, the impact on adolescent attendance held steady at three years—the detail that carries weight for a business case and the one typically skipped in the overview. The transport gain held steady as well. A policy that secures a lasting benefit with a lasting expense is a wholly different thing from one that secures a lasting benefit with a single payment, and the paperwork seldom clarifies which category applies.

In September 2017, Kelso County altered its rural broadband subsidy, and the impact on new business creation outpaced everything the authority had financed in the ten years before. The result held up in two separate replications, yet it failed in one district that had already shifted its own scheduling ahead of the study.

A 2019 examination of 26 districts arrived at the same conclusion through an entirely separate methodology. The authors stated that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a claim that advocates and critics alike have cited repeatedly, sometimes with the final phrase stripped away. What the review truly proposed was a gradual implementation paired with a comparison group, a step no governing body has taken to date.

The minority opinion deserves a full citation, as it is often watered down in summary. The dissenting report put it this way:

    purchased at the price stated. The civil works line rose in the first

The infrastructure accounting tells the remaining story, and that story surfaces first. The uptick in civil construction likewise persisted. A measure delivering a lasting benefit at a lasting expense is a very different thing from one that secures a lasting benefit at a one-time price, and the reports seldom distinguish between the two.

Conclusion

The attendance figure for adolescents is genuine, so is the duration figure, and so is the cost of transport. The only stance that fails honesty is citing two of those three. Bodies weighing the change should start with the transport line when pricing it, given that it lands on day one and never leaves.
