1. Fairmont: School start times

In September 2016, Fairmont adjusted its school schedules, producing a shift in teen attendance unlike any intervention the district had backed in the prior ten years. Over the initial twelve months, attendance among adolescents rose by eight percent relative to a benchmark of fifteen comparable districts, and that advantage persisted even after three years had passed. No one contests the measurement—only its significance.

Smith's 2018 analysis framed the outcome with blunt clarity, and that single line—found on page thirty-eight - has appeared in every funding proposal since: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget". The finding held up through two independent validations and one inconclusive test in a district that had recently overhauled its own schedule.

A separate 2018 assessment covering twenty-three districts arrived at an identical verdict through a different method. Its researchers wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow", though the full statement - often truncated when cited - actually called for a phased introduction alongside a control group, a strategy no district has yet adopted.

The opposing viewpoint, frequently softened in retellings, deserves to be presented exactly as written. The dissenting section stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The movement records reveal the prior chapter of this account - the chapter that unfolds ahead of the rest. Fairmont's operations split into two distinct levels, and the adjustment forced both levels into overlapping schedules. The agency acquired 4 vehicles and brought on 6 employees, which drove transport usage up by roughly 10 percent in year one and an additional 4 percent in year two. These shifts are absent from the youth participation data but fully documented in the financial reports.

"The funds were allocated from the same source regardless," the financial director stated, a point no one in the room challenged. Another official noted that this source had been exhausted since 2012. Neither statement was included in the official minutes, which simply stated that the committee had acknowledged the document.

The 2022 reassessment introduced a critical factor: time. In 10 of the 15 regions, the impact on youth participation remained just as strong after three years - a detail essential for justifying investment yet frequently omitted from summaries. The transport expansion also held steady. A strategy that secures lasting benefits at a sustained expense differs fundamentally from one that achieves the same with a single payment, though the documentation seldom clarifies the distinction.

2. Kelso County: Rural broadband subsidy

Kelso County altered its rural broadband funding in September 2017, producing a surge in small business creation unlike anything the local government had achieved in the prior ten years. Within the first full year, new business formation climbed by 9 percent compared to a control set of 16 comparable regions, and the advantage persisted unchanged three years on. The accuracy of the number is uncontested; its significance is not.

Back in 2019, Harrison framed the finding with blunt clarity, producing a line so definitive that it now appears in virtually every subsequent business analysis: "the change in rural broadband subsidy did more for small business formation than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2013 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Harrison, 2019, p. 45). That assertion held firm through two successful repetitions and one unsuccessful attempt in an area where schedules had already been altered independently.

A separate examination from 2019, covering twenty-six districts, landed on the identical verdict but through a distinct approach. The researchers behind it wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" - a statement that has since been cited by advocates and critics alike, though often with the latter half omitted. Their actual proposal called for a gradual introduction alongside a comparison group, a strategy no jurisdiction has implemented to date.

The opposing view deserves to be presented exactly as written, since it is routinely softened in retellings. The dissenting section of the report stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The civil works line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

Conclusion

The uptick in teenage participation is undeniable, the effect on time spent is undeniable, and the expense for transportation is undeniable. Any argument that acknowledges only two while ignoring the third is fundamentally misleading. Officials considering the shift ought to calculate the transit costs upfront, given that they materialize immediately and persist indefinitely.
