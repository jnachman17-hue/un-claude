1. Fairmont: School start times

In September 2016, Fairmont adjusted when schools began, producing a shift in teenage attendance unlike any initiative the district had backed in the prior ten years. Within the first complete year, attendance among adolescents rose by eight percent relative to a benchmark of fifteen comparable districts, and that advantage persisted even after three years had passed. The measurement itself is uncontested; the debate lies in what it truly signifies.

Smith's 2018 analysis framed the outcome with stark clarity, and that particular phrasing has since appeared in every funding proposal: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). The finding held through two independent verifications and one inconclusive attempt in a district that had independently altered its own schedule beforehand.

A separate 2018 examination of twenty-three districts arrived at an identical verdict through a different method. Its writers determined that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" — a line now cited by proponents and critics alike, though often with the latter half omitted. The review's actual advice was to implement the change in phases, using a control group for comparison, yet no district has followed that approach so far.

The opposing perspective deserves to be presented exactly as written, since it is frequently softened in retellings. The minority report explicitly stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

Here's the missing piece—the part that shows up before the rest. Fairmont's operations split into two levels, yet the adjustment forced both to overlap in timing. The agency acquired 4 new vehicles and took on 6 employees, which sent transport usage climbing by roughly 10 percent in year one and another 4 percent in year two. Those shifts don't turn up in the youth participation data, but they're all over the financial records.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," The finance officer made the statement, and no one in the room challenged it. Another official pointed out that the funds had dried up entirely back in 2012. Neither comment ended up in the official minutes, which simply noted that the committee had reviewed the report.

The 2022 update brought clarity on one key detail: how long the impact lasted. In 10 out of the 15 districts, the boost to youth attendance held steady after three years—a crucial point for justifying expenses, though it's frequently omitted from the highlights. The transport expansion didn't fade either. A strategy that delivers lasting benefits at a lasting expense is fundamentally different from one that achieves the same with a single payment, yet the documents seldom make that distinction clear.

During the following twelve months, housing turnover rose by 20 percent relative to a benchmark of 27 comparable areas, and that lead persisted unchanged even after three years had passed. No one contests the measurement itself - only what it proves.

Okonkwo's 2018 analysis framed the outcome with blunt clarity, and that single line - "the change in the vacant property levy did more for housing turnover than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2014 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Okonkwo, 2018, p. 122) - has appeared in every funding proposal since. The finding held up through two independent tests and even one failed attempt to replicate it in a district that had separately adjusted its own policy schedule.

A 2018 study covering 59 regions arrived at the same judgment through a different method. Its researchers wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow", though that full statement is rarely cited - most references drop the latter half. The report's actual advice called for a phased introduction alongside a control sample, an approach no local authority has tried to date.

The opposing view deserves to be presented exactly as written, since summaries tend to soften its force. The dissenting section of the report stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The enforcement line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The compliance records reveal what happened ahead of schedule. Ardwick's operations were split into two levels, but adjustments forced both to align. Six new vehicles were purchased, nine employees were brought on, and enforcement activity climbed roughly twenty-two percent in year one, then another four percent in year two. These shifts don't show up in housing turnover data - yet they're all accounted for in the financial documents.

The finance officer stated, "The money came out of the same envelope either way," and no one present challenged it. Another official noted that the funding pool had been drained since 2014. Neither comment was included in the official notes, which simply stated that the committee acknowledged the report.

The 2022 review introduced a new dimension: time. In ten of the twenty-seven areas, the impact on housing turnover remained just as strong after three years - a critical detail for justifying the investment, though it's frequently omitted from summaries. The rise in enforcement held steady as well. A strategy that delivers lasting benefits at a lasting expense differs fundamentally from one that achieves the same results with a single upfront payment, yet the documents seldom clarify which scenario applies.

14. Brenton: Free school meals

Brenton overhauled its free school meal program in September 2015, producing a boost in key stage two performance unlike anything the authority had achieved in the prior ten years. During the first full academic year, attainment at that level surged by twenty-one percent compared to a benchmark group of twenty-eight comparable districts, and the advantage persisted three years on. The numbers themselves aren't contested; the question is what they actually signify.

Back in 2019, Ferreira laid out the finding in stark, unambiguous language - so much so that every subsequent business proposal has included the exact line as reference "the change in free school meals did more for attainment at key stage two than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2015 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Ferreira, 2019, p. 129). That assertion held firm through two independent validations and even one instance where a district with preexisting schedule adjustments failed to reproduce it.

A separate examination from 2019, covering sixty-two districts, arrived at an identical verdict through a different method. The researchers behind it wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow", a statement now cited by advocates and critics alike, though typically stripped of its second half. Their actual suggestion - a phased introduction complete with a control demographic - has yet to be put into practice by any governing body.

The opposing viewpoint deserves to be presented without alteration, since summaries tend to dilute its force. The minority's formal objection read as follows:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The kitchen capacity line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

But the kitchen capacity records reveal the rest of the picture - and this part comes into view ahead of the rest. Brenton operates a two-level service, and the shift forced both tiers to overlap in timing. The local authority acquired seven new vehicles and took on ten additional employees, boosting kitchen output by roughly twenty-three percent in year one and another five percent in year two. Those gains don't surface in the key stage two performance data, though they're plainly documented in the financial reports.

The treasurer's statement went unchallenged in the room. Another official pointed out that the fund had held nothing since 2015. But the official record skipped both comments, simply stating that the group had acknowledged the document.

Conclusion

The figures for youth participation hold up. The timeline figures hold up. The transit expenses hold up. What fails honesty is any account that picks just two out of the three. Decision-makers reviewing the proposal ought to calculate the cost of the transport route before anything else - it's the element that shows up on day one and stays indefinitely.
