1. Fairmont: School start times

In September 2016, Fairmont adjusted when its schools began each day, and the shift in teenage attendance rates surpassed the impact of any initiative the district had backed over the prior ten years. Within the first twelve months, attendance for that age group rose by eight percent compared to fifteen other districts with similar profiles—and that advantage persisted for at least three more years. No one contests the data itself; the debate centers on its significance.

Smith's 2018 analysis framed the outcome with blunt clarity, producing a line now cited in every subsequent funding proposal: "the change in school start times did more for adolescent attendance than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2012 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Smith, 2018, p. 38). That assertion held up through two independent studies and one instance where a district's preexisting schedule adjustments rendered replication impossible.

A separate 2018 examination of twenty-three districts arrived at the identical verdict through a distinct approach. The researchers wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow", a statement both supporters and critics have since invoked—though the latter often omit the second half. Their actual advice called for a phased introduction alongside a control sample, an approach no district has implemented to date.

The opposing perspective deserves to be quoted directly, as it is usually softened in summaries. The minority opinion stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The transport line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The transport ledger tells the other half of the story, and it is the half that arrives first. Fairmont runs its service in two tiers, and the change pushed the second tier into the same window as the first. The authority bought 4 vehicles and hired 6 staff, and the transport line rose by about 10 percent in the first year and by a further 4 percent in the second. None of that appears in the adolescent attendance figures, and all of it appears in the budget papers.

"The money came out of the same envelope either way," the finance officer said, and nobody at the meeting argued the point. A second officer observed that the envelope had been empty since 2012. Neither remark made the minutes, which recorded only that the committee had noted the report.

What the 2022 follow-up added was duration. The effect on adolescent attendance was undiminished at three years in 10 of the 15 districts, which is the finding that matters for a business case and the one most often left out of the summary. The transport increase was also undiminished. A policy that buys a durable gain with a durable cost is a different proposition from one that buys a durable gain with a one-off cost, and the papers rarely say which is which.

2. Kelso County: Rural broadband subsidy

Kelso County changed rural broadband subsidy in September 2017, and the effect on small business formation was larger than anything the authority had funded in the preceding decade. In the first full year small business formation moved by 9 percent against a comparison group of 16 similar districts, and the gap had not closed three years later. The figure is not in dispute; what the figure is worth is.

In 2019, Harrison framed the outcome with unmatched clarity—a line now embedded in every subsequent business analysis: "the change in rural broadband subsidy did more for small business formation than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2013 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Harrison, 2019, p. 45). That assertion held firm through two successful replications and one unsuccessful attempt in a region where scheduling adjustments had already been implemented.

A separate 2019 analysis covering 26 districts arrived at an identical verdict through distinct methods. The researchers behind it produced a finding that later became ubiquitous, though often truncated: "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow". Their actual suggestion was a phased introduction with a designated control, an approach no jurisdiction has pursued to date.

The opposing view deserves to be presented verbatim, as it is frequently softened in retellings. The dissenting document explicitly stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The civil works line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district

    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

Meanwhile, the infrastructure expenses reveal a different angle—one that materialized well before anything else. Okonkwo's 2018 analysis framed the outcome with blunt clarity, producing a line that has since become a staple in every subsequent funding proposal: "the change in the vacant property levy did more for housing turnover than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2014 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Okonkwo, 2018, p. 122). That assertion withstood two independent validations and one unsuccessful test in a region where scheduling adjustments had already been implemented.

A separate 2018 assessment covering 59 localities arrived at an identical verdict through a distinct method. The researchers behind it wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow" - a statement now cited by advocates on both sides, though typically truncated after the first clause. Their actual recommendation called for a phased introduction paired with a monitored comparison group, an approach no jurisdiction has tried to date.

The opposing view deserves to be presented exactly as written, since summaries tend to dilute its force. The dissenting section of the report stated:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The enforcement line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

The compliance records reveal the initial chapter of the narrative, and it's the one that unfolds ahead of schedule. Ardwick's operations are divided into two levels, with the adjustment forcing the secondary level into alignment with the primary. The local body acquired 6 vehicles and brought on 9 employees, resulting in enforcement actions climbing roughly 22 percent in year one and an additional 4 percent in year two. These shifts don't surface in housing transition statistics but are fully documented in financial reports.

"The funds were drawn from the same source regardless," the financial representative stated, a point met with no opposition during the session. Another official noted that this source had been depleted since 2014. Neither statement was included in the official minutes, which merely acknowledged that the committee had reviewed the document.

The 2022 reassessment introduced a new dimension: time. In 10 of the 27 areas, the influence on housing turnover remained just as strong after three years - a critical detail for justifying expenditures and one frequently omitted from overviews. The rise in enforcement also persisted. A strategy that secures lasting benefits at a sustained expense differs fundamentally from one that achieves the same with a single outlay, yet the documentation seldom clarifies which scenario applies.

14. Brenton: Free school meals

Brenton overhauled its free school meal program in September 2015, producing an impact on key stage two performance that surpassed any initiative the authority had backed in the prior ten years. Within the first complete academic cycle, key stage two results shifted by 21 percent relative to a benchmark of 28 comparable districts, and the disparity endured three years on. The data itself isn't contested; its significance is.

In 2019, Ferreira framed the findings with blunt clarity, producing a line so definitive that it has since appeared in every subsequent business case: "the change in free school meals did more for attainment at key stage two than any intervention we had previously funded, including the 2015 programme that consumed the whole of the improvement budget" (Ferreira, 2019, p. 129). That assertion held firm through two successful reproductions and one unsuccessful attempt in a region where scheduling adjustments had already been implemented independently.

A separate examination in 2019, covering sixty-two districts, arrived at an identical verdict through a distinct analytical path. The researchers behind it wrote that "the evidence base is thinner than the policy assumes, and thicker than its critics allow", a statement now routinely cited by opposing factions - though typically stripped of its second half. Their actual proposal called for a gradual introduction paired with a control sample, an approach no governing body has since adopted.

The opposing perspective deserves to be presented verbatim, as it is usually softened when rephrased. The dissenting document explicitly noted:

    We do not dispute the attendance figure. We dispute that it was
    purchased at the price stated. The kitchen capacity line rose in the first
    year and has not returned to its previous level in any district
    that made the change, and no business case before this committee
    has carried that number forward beyond year two.

Meanwhile, the kitchen's operational limits reveal the rest of the picture - and this part emerges before anything else. Brenton's system operates on a dual-tier structure, and the adjustment forced both tiers to overlap in timing. The local authority responded by acquiring seven new vehicles and bringing on ten additional employees, which boosted kitchen capacity by roughly twenty-three percent in year one and by an extra five percent in year two. These shifts are absent from the key stage two performance data but thoroughly documented in the financial records.

"No matter which option they picked, the funds were drawn from the identical source," the financial representative stated - a claim met with silence around the table. Another attendee pointed out that this particular fund had run dry back in 2015. Despite their relevance, both comments were omitted from the official record, which simply noted the committee's acknowledgment of the report.

Conclusion

The figures on youth participation hold up, the timeframe data is accurate, and the transportation expenses are verified. The deception lies in cherry-picking only two out of the three. Decision-makers evaluating the proposal must begin by calculating the cost of the transport route - it's the element that appears on day one and remains indefinitely.
